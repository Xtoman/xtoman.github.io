Employment isn't a stepping stone to freedom — it's a carefully furnished cage. It can be very comfortable: a predictable salary, a familiar schedule, clear scenarios for the year ahead. But the limit hides in that very predictability — on a salary, it's nearly impossible to grow to the point where you can simply disappear for three months, devote yourself entirely to a hobby, and not think about money. The system pays enough so you don't starve, but not enough to truly afford not being in the system.

The logical answer to this sense of deadlock seems to be entrepreneurship: as if only owning a business gives you a chance to break free from the wheel. But entrepreneurship is impossible without capital, and capital isn't needed for a glamorous launch — it's needed for basic survival: food, somewhere to live, paying bills while your venture learns to feed you on its own. The paradox is that most entrepreneurs, on average, earn no more — and often less — over the long run than skilled employees, only with far higher risk and stress.

Employment provides the one valuable thing you need to start a business — a stable cash flow. You can plan a budget, save, invest, think about a "safety cushion," but the price is time: after work hours and commuting, there's neither energy nor mental space left to build something of your own. The result is that you have money you could invest in an idea, but no living person who's willing to work a second shift after the workday for an uncertain future.

A theoretical way out remains: if you don't have time but you have money, you need to find someone who has time but no money. That is, effectively buy someone else's freedom so they'll build your business while you cover their basic needs with your salary. But then the question is who actually becomes the entrepreneur — you, who pays, or them, who lives in that risk and uncertainty every day. The reverse isn't any better: if you go into business yourself, your bills, family, and obligations don't disappear — someone else has to stay in the system and keep playing at stability.

There's no elegant exit in this construction: employment consumes time, entrepreneurship consumes security, and attempts to combine them turn into slow burnout between two logics. Money buys time, but to get money, you sell the very time you're short of — a closed loop where every choice is already a loss in its own way. Perhaps the only honest position here is to admit that the system is built so you almost never get a calm way out of it, and from that sense of hopelessness, if any strength remains, try to find your own private, unglamorous, compromise way out.
